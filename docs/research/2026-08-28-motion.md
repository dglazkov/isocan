# Motion on the canvas: three kinds, and only one of them is missing

**28 August 2026**

**Where this stands, 29 Aug 2026: not built, and one thing changed around
it.** Pan inertia and arrival motion are both still unbuilt. What did land is
motion of the third kind the doc names — state made legible: the rail's
open/close now PANS the canvas rather than covering it, and that pan is a
compositor transform transition after a `requestAnimationFrame` version
arrived in visible chunks. Worth reading beside recommendation 1, because it
is the same argument about who owns the frame.

The question: *should we add extra touches of velocity and animation to canvas
interactions?* — pointed at a post on X that could not be read (403 to an
unauthenticated fetch, and no description of it found by search). So this is
the question researched from the code out, and if that post showed something
specific it may well name a fourth thing this misses.

The short version: **isocan already has more considered motion than the
question assumes**, and the one real gap is the kind that is hardest to
justify — motion that decides where things end up.

## Three kinds of motion, which is the distinction that matters

Reading what is already here, the codebase has been sorting motion into two
buckets without naming them. Naming them is what makes the third one's
argument possible.

**1. Motion as decoration.** An entrance; a hover; a sheen. It can be skipped
and nothing is lost. The rule is already written, in the toast's own CSS:
*"The settled state IS the base state; the keyframes are only an entrance, so
a throttled tab (animations paused) still shows a readable toast."* Forty
transitions and animations in the stylesheet live here, with seven
`prefers-reduced-motion` guards over the ones that would be unpleasant.

**2. Motion as data.** Not decoration at all — it is how a discontinuous
signal is made legible. Two examples, both already built and both better than
the question presumes:

- **The camera.** `glideTo` eases a viewport change over a cubic in-out, and
  its comment states the reason exactly: *"a jump across the canvas reads as
  travel rather than teleportation — you keep your bearings because you saw
  which way you went."* A second glide cancels the first, and
  `prefers-reduced-motion` arrives instantly instead.
- **Other people's cursors.** Presence is sampled at 33ms
  (`PRESENCE_INTERVAL_MS`), so a remote cursor is a stream of discrete points;
  drawn raw it stutters. `CursorLayer` lerps each one toward its target every
  frame — and at **different rates by who is moving it**: `LERP_HUMAN = 0.22`
  because a real cursor tracks tightly, `LERP_AGENT = 0.11` because a CLI hop
  should read as deliberate rather than as a jump cut, and `WANDER_LERP = 0.07`
  with a deliberate sub-pixel jitter while an agent is working — *"a hand, not
  a tween"*. That is characterisation, not smoothing, and there is nothing to
  add to it.

**3. Motion that decides state.** Inertia. A flick that keeps travelling
after you let go does not merely animate a change — it *chooses* one. This is
the kind isocan does not have, and the kind that needs the tightest argument,
because the rule that governs the other two ("the settled state is the base
state; the motion is skippable") cannot govern it: the settled state is
exactly what the motion produces.

## What is missing, and what should stay missing

**Pan inertia — yes, narrowly.** Dragging with the Hand tool or the middle
button stops dead on release. Every canvas people arrive from — Figma, Miro,
tldraw, every map — coasts, and on an infinite canvas the alternative is
drag-lift-drag-lift to cross open space. Trackpad users already get momentum
free, because a two-finger scroll arrives as decaying wheel events from the
OS; **the people without it are mouse users and anyone on the Hand tool**,
which is a real and unbalanced gap rather than a missing flourish.

The conditions it has to meet, which follow from it being kind 3:

- **Interruptible.** A press during the coast stops it where it is. Nobody
  should have to wait for the canvas to finish moving.
- **Gentle, and short.** This is a canvas people align things on. Coasting
  past a target is worse than stopping short, so the decay should end travel
  in a few hundred milliseconds, not carry across screens.
- **Reduced motion turns it off, and that changes the outcome** — which is
  the honest cost and should be stated rather than smoothed over. It is
  defensible only because the flick's *committed* part is the movement the
  hand actually made; inertia continues it, and continuing less is a smaller
  difference than an animation somebody cannot see at all.

**Throwing items — no.** Coasting an item to rest is the same trick applied
to a canvas FACT: an item's position is an op, it replicates, `⌘Z` reaches
it, `nearestFreeSpot` and the snapping guides all have opinions about it. A
gesture whose outcome is "roughly there, depending on how hard you flicked"
makes a shared, versioned number imprecise, and it would fight three
mechanisms that exist to make placement deliberate. Fuzz is not a feature in
a value other people read.

**Arrival of a remote item — worth a small yes.** A teammate's screen
currently appears fully formed between one frame and the next. A short fade
or scale-in is kind 1, costs nothing, is skippable, and makes a canvas that
several people are working on feel inhabited rather than glitchy. It is the
cheapest item here.

**Rubber-banding at the edges — not applicable.** The canvas is infinite;
there is no edge to band against.

## Recommendation

1. **Pan inertia on the Hand tool and middle-drag**, under the three
   conditions above. It is the only real gap, and it is a gap because mouse
   users pay for it while trackpad users never noticed.
2. **Arrival motion for items that appear from somebody else.** Small, kind 1,
   improves the thing this product is actually about.
3. **Nothing for items being dragged by hand.** Their position is a fact, and
   the canvas already has three mechanisms making it a deliberate one.

And the finding worth keeping regardless of what gets built: the reason this
codebase's motion holds together is that it has never animated for its own
sake. Every existing piece is either skippable or is making a discrete signal
readable. A fourth category — motion that decides state — is a real addition
to that vocabulary and should be added once, deliberately, rather than
arriving as a flourish.
