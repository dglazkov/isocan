Sorted, like the WYSIWYG note, by the one observation no generic "add a
timeline" comparison covers: **isocan already is a timeline**, and has been
since the first reducer. The oplog is an ordered list of operations; the
canvas at any moment is the fold of that list up to a sequence number. A
scrubber is not a feature bolted onto that — it is a second reader of the
thing the whole system is built out of. What follows is what that makes free,
what it makes hard, and where the real design choices actually are.

## The isocan-shaped problem

Three facts already in the codebase decide almost everything:

1. **The reducer is pure and per-op** (`core/reducer.ts`, `applyOperation`).
   The canvas at seq *N* is `ops[1..N]` folded through it — deterministic, no
   clock, no network. "Show me the canvas as of seq N" is already answerable;
   nothing new has to be invented to compute a past frame.
2. **Every LogEntry carries its own inverse** (`core/invert.ts`; the daemon
   computes it before applying and stores it, never re-derives it — it is
   what per-actor undo replays). So the fold is walkable in *both*
   directions: forward is apply-the-op, backward is apply-the-stored-inverse.
   A scrubber that steps one seq does O(1) work, not an O(N) replay.
3. **The full history is now reachable end to end.** `gc` compacts old ops
   into an archive but never deletes them, and as of today `readArchivedLog`
   + the live log is the complete record (the archive/recap work, same day).
   A timeline can honestly reach seq 1 even on a long-lived canvas, past
   every compaction — which is the difference between a timeline and a
   fading memory.

And one fact decides the *architecture*:

4. **A timeline sends nothing.** It is read-only over the oplog. The
   isomorphism law — every shared fact is an operation either surface can
   send — is satisfied trivially, because a timeline asserts no facts. There
   are **zero new op types**, the same result the whole workbench reached,
   and for a stronger reason: there is nothing here to make an op *of*. The
   parity that must hold is over the **address** — a seq — not over pixels.

## What the seq being the address buys

A timeline position is a single integer: a seq. That lands it squarely in the
**routes ledger** (`address.ts`, the one-spelling rule), exactly where the
item-cover and workbench-cover addresses already live. `…/i/:itemId` gains an
`at=<seq>` the way the covers gained their paths; the CLI already speaks the
same address (`isocan tail --archived`, `isocan recap`, and a natural
`isocan at <seq>` that reduces and prints). One position, two surfaces,
no divergence — the isomorphism holds not because we were careful but because
there was only ever one number to agree on.

Presence is the honest exception, and worth stating so nobody chases it: the
ephemeral plane (who is here, who is working) is **never replayed** — it is
client-asserted and lives in daemon memory only. A past frame therefore shows
*structure and content*, never live cursors. Authorship survives (every op
carries its actor), so "who changed this, when" is fully recoverable; "whose
cursor was where at 3pm last Tuesday" is gone, and correctly so.

## The field, surveyed

### A. The version-stack scrubber (the entry point alone)

The stage already stacks `item.addVersion` versions (`v1…vN`, ⌘S mints one).
Scrubbing *one artifact's own versions* is a slider over that stack —
cheap, local, and half-built. **Critique:** it is the "main entry point
changing" half of the ask and nothing more. It cannot show the canvas
growing around the artifact, and it has no notion of a major change — every
version is one tick. Right answer for "how did this screen evolve", wrong
scope for "scrub the canvas through time". Ship it as the *focused* mode, not
the whole feature.

### B. Full-canvas time-travel by reduce-to-seq

Point the stage/viewport at `reduce(ops[1..N])` and let a scrubber move N.
This is the isomorphic-pure win in its strongest form: the entire board —
every item's position, every version current *as of then*, every comment
thread's state — reconstructed exactly. **Critique:** the cost is the fold.
Naively, each scrub tick re-reduces from seq 1, which is O(N) per frame and
janky on a big canvas. Two mitigations, both already latent in the system:
step incrementally using the **stored inverses** (fact 2) so a one-seq move
is one op applied, not a replay; and seek from the **nearest snapshot** the
store already keeps (`compactOplog` retains one; load replays only the
lagging tail) rather than from the origin. Neither is new machinery — they
are the undo path and the crash-recovery path, read for a second purpose.

### C. The op-stream log (git-log --graph, in the canvas)

A scrollable list of operations with authors and times. **Critique:** this is
`isocan tail` and `recap` — it already exists, and it is *information, not a
timeline*. A person asked for scrubbing because a list of 4,000 `item.move`
lines is exactly what they do not want to read. Valuable as the **detail
view** a marker expands into ("what actually happened in this span"), never
as the top-level surface. Its rendering is already written; reuse it there.

### D. Hybrid — a scrub track with marked majors, driving reduce-to-seq

A slim time track along the bottom of the cover: a draggable playhead over
the seq range, **major changes as labeled ticks**, scrubbing drives option
B's reduce-to-seq, with the entry point as the default focus (option A's
view) and a zoom-out to the whole canvas. A tick expands to option C's op
list for its span. **Critique:** it is the most work, and it is the only
option that answers the ask as asked — "the canvas *and/or* the main entry
point," "major points for major changes." The and/or is a focus toggle, not
two features; the majors are the open question below.

## What "major" means, and where it must live

"Major points for major changes" is a **significance function over
LogEntry[]**, and the one firm rule is that it belongs in **core**, pure, the
way `buildRecap` already is — because the CLI must mark the same majors the
web does, or the two surfaces disagree about what mattered, which is the one
thing this architecture does not permit. Web renders them as ticks; the CLI
renders them as `recap` lines (it half-does already — recap's window
boundaries are a first, coarse answer to "where are the seams").

Three candidate definitions, in increasing fidelity:

- **Structural ops outrank churn.** `item.add` (a birth), `item.delete`, a
  version minted on the focused artifact, the first comment on a thread,
  `thread.setMain` — these are majors; a run of `item.move` is one minor
  ripple, not forty ticks. A weight per op type, summed per candidate window,
  is the cheap first cut and is probably 80% right.
- **Recap windows as the coarse track.** `buildRecap` already carves the past
  into spans that double as they recede, each with a seq range and a
  who/what/how-much summary. Those boundaries *are* a zoom-dependent set of
  majors, for free — the timeline's far-back region can be recap windows and
  its near region individual majors, which is also the right visual density.
- **Named checkpoints (later, and only if wanted).** A person marking "this
  is the one we shipped" would be the sole case that *does* want an op — and
  it is the same shape as reactions/marks (a stamp on a seq), so if it ever
  lands it lands as that, not as timeline-specific machinery. Deliberately
  out of scope until asked; noted so it is a decision, not a surprise.

## Recommendation

**D, staged onto what exists: version-stack scrubber (A) first as the focused
mode; then reduce-to-seq (B) for the whole-canvas mode, stepping via stored
inverses and seeking from the store's snapshot; markers from a pure core
significance function seeded by recap's windows (C reused as the detail
view).**

The reasoning in one paragraph: a timeline is the rare feature that is *pure
gain* against isocan's grain rather than across it — it sends nothing, invents
no op, and its position is a seq that both surfaces already speak. Every hard
part has an answer already sitting in the tree for another reason: the fold is
the reducer, bidirectional stepping is the undo inverses, deep history is the
new archive, the coarse markers are recap's windows, and the detail view is
`tail`. The work is not mechanism; it is **rendering and performance** — a
scrub track, a focus toggle, and making reduce-to-seq smooth by reading the
snapshot and the inverses that already exist. That is the same shape the
workbench turned out to be: no new vocabulary, a new *view* of the vocabulary
there was.

## Open questions, for the build

- **Where the reduce happens.** Client-side keeps the loop local (the tab
  already holds the oplog it is watching) and is the default answer;
  daemon-side `isocan at <seq>` is a natural CLI verb and a small pure
  handler over the same fold. Both, probably — the core function is shared,
  each surface calls it where it already has the ops.
- **Snapshot seek granularity.** The store keeps one snapshot per canvas
  (the compaction boundary). Scrubbing far *behind* that boundary replays
  from the archive; whether that wants periodic snapshots (every K ops) is a
  perf question to answer with a real long canvas, not before. The archive
  read is O(history) once, cached per session — likely enough.
- **The focus toggle's default.** "Entry point" needs a designated primary
  artifact; a canvas may have none, one, or several screens. The likely
  answer is the item currently on the stage (the cover already knows it), with
  the whole-canvas zoom-out as the fallback when nothing is focused.
- **Playback vs. scrub.** A play button (advance seq on a clock) is a small
  addition once scrubbing works, and it is where a timeline becomes a
  *story* — the diverge/converge history of a canvas, watched. Out of the
  first cut, but the track should be built so a playhead can be driven by a
  timer as easily as by a drag.
