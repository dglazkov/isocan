# 12 September 2026

## The suite's one source of random red, named

`packages/cli/test/rc.test.ts` reddened five releases on 11 September, on
five unrelated commits — two of them docs-only pull requests, which is how
you know the change never caused it. Every one was the same case, "the web's
withdraw, seen by a parked rc", failing on the same assertion, with output
that stopped dead one line short:

```
AssertionError: expected 'rc: agents that named no harness run …'
  to match /Percy · pass pss_\S+ was never redeem…/
…
Percy · pasture isocan-percy stays — it is yours     ← the output ends here
```

Not a product race. `withdrawSheep` narrates in two halves: `endSheep` ends
the sheep and signs off with *"pasture … stays — it is yours"*, and only then
does `endCellBadge` ask the home which badge redeemed the cell's pass — an
HTTP round trip, and a line of its own. The test waited for the pasture line
and asserted on the badge line in the next statement, so its window was
exactly one round trip wide: invisible on a laptop, routine on a shared
runner. The wait was one line short, and because the assertion was right the
failure read as the product being broken.

A second case had the same shape a day earlier: *"with nothing settling it"*
waited for the rc's *"answering on …"* — printed near the top of a start
whose cursor claims land several round trips later — then asked `isocan who`
once and expected Sian to read `answerable`. The table printed; her row just
still said `enrolled`.

Both were reproduced character for character by forcing the window with a
`setTimeout` inside the product (400 ms before the badge lookup, 1500 ms
before the claims), and both survive that forcing with the sentinel moved.
Nothing is slept on, nothing is retried, no timeout is widened: each wait now
names the END of its sequence — the badge line, and the daemon's own
`rcAnswering` set, which is the fact `isocan who` renders as `answerable`.
The three sibling cases that stop at the pasture line were never at risk and
use the same sentinel anyway, because one sequence deserves one sentinel.

The third failure in the record — "a web add gets its rc half" timing out at
20 s — was a real product race, and was already fixed the same morning
(`37c7359a`, lesson #50): the window between the roster read and the start
tip.

Twelve runs of the repaired file under 12x CPU oversubscription then found
the same mistake wearing its other face: **asserting on one writer's output
when the product deliberately lets two race.** `withdrawSheep` says in as
many words that a verb and a parked rc both arrive at a withdrawal and the
second finds the sheep already gone — and "mid-turn at a station too old to
end a sheep" read *"its running turn was aborted"* off the verb alone. One
failure in twelve, with the verb's output complete and correct and the abort
simply not its to report. Its sibling case already read that line across both
outputs; both now wait for it across both.

[lessons.md #53](../reviews/lessons.md) states the shape: **the sentinel is
the last thing the sequence says, not the last thing you happened to
notice**; when the wait is for a fact rather than a line, wait for the fact;
and assert on what is decided, wait for what is not.

The same campaign turned up one failure that is **not** this and is not
fixed here: a spawned rc's `fetch` to its own daemon failing eight seconds
after a summons, which is [the flake family's](../research/2026-08-29-the-flake-family.md)
own 7.8 s loopback signature arriving where its 3 September fix does not
reach — that connect deadline was given to the suite's HTTP client, and
every process the suite starts has its own. The product survived it (the rc
retries in 60 s); only the test's deadline did not, which is not a reason to
widen a test. Recorded in the note, unfixed, and never seen on CI, where the
load is a quarter of this.

## One rc file becomes three

The other half of that file's reputation was cost: 42 tests, each starting a
daemon and several processes, all in one file — and vitest runs a file's
tests one after another. 90 s on a laptop, up to 126 s on CI, the slowest
file in the suite by a wide margin and all of it on one core while the
others idled.

It is now `rc.test.ts`, `rc-sheep.test.ts` and `rc-sheep-withdrawal.test.ts`
over two fixture modules, `rc-fixture.ts` and `sheep-fixture.ts`, holding the
scratch home, the daemon, the badge and the helpers. The shared values are
exported `let` bindings, so every test reads the same names it always did.
Nothing weakens — each test always had its own home and daemon and never saw
another's, and the describe names are unchanged. **90.6 s → 39.3 s** wall for
the same 42 tests.

`test/teardown.test.ts` now walks the helper modules beside the test files,
not only `*.test.ts`. Moving an `afterEach` into a fixture would otherwise
have carried the `fs.rm` that lesson #42 exists for out from under its own
guard, and a guard you can step out from under by extracting a function is
not one.

## Seen-marks: one fact, read two ways

Two features had been stopped on the same undecided question for a fortnight.
[The inbox](../research/2026-08-29-the-inbox.md) (#147) could list what was
addressed to you but not say what was **new**, because "what I have read" lived
in `localStorage` — so a count meant "in this browser", and a mention on a
canvas this browser had never opened, which is precisely the case an inbox is
for, was invisible to it. [The switcher](../projects/switcher/design.md) (#134)
led with the canvases you were on lately, and that list was per browser too, so
a person on a laptop and a desktop had two of them.

Dion decided on 11 September that a new op was acceptable *if one was genuinely
needed*, and — the load-bearing half — that the two had to be designed
**together**, because deciding them apart is how a product ends up with two
mechanisms for one fact. [The design](../research/2026-09-12-seen-marks.md) and
the build are both from today.

**One fact.** `(person, canvas) → { seq, at }`, written when you OPEN a canvas.
Because it is written on the visit it means two things at once — *I was here at
`at`*, and *everything up to `seq` was in front of me* — and that is why one row
serves both readers rather than two rows serving one each. The inbox reads the
second half; the switcher's "lately" reads the first. The rule that keeps it
honest is one line and it is in the code: **only a visit writes a mark.** A
background sweep or a "mark all read" button would move `at` without anybody
having been there, and "lately" would fill with canvases nobody went to.

**Both a seq and a timestamp, because neither can do the other's job.** The seq
answers *has anything happened here* against `lastSeq` in one integer, with no
clock and no scan — but a `Comment` carries `createdAt` and not the seq of the
op that wrote it, so it cannot say which comment is new. The instant answers
exactly that, and is the only half comparable across canvases, which is what
"lately" is ordered by. Each is monotonic on its own, so a revisit that changed
nothing still moves `at` — the canvas you read for an hour and touched nothing
on is exactly the one you come back to.

**No new op. The vocabulary stayed at 33.** The permission was there and was not
used, which is worth as much as a raise would have been. The invariant that
holds for all thirty-three is that an op is appended to a log *and* its effect is
visible to everyone who can see the thing it is about; a seen-mark breaks both
halves deliberately — nobody else may see what you have read, and a log line per
glance is attention replicating. An op would have needed exemptions from
replication, from undo, from the wire shapes and from the log itself: four
exceptions to the definition of an op, which is another way of spelling "this is
not an op". The clinching test was the context project's own three, inherited by
[memory](../projects/memory/design.md): can it be undone, can everyone see it,
does it work with the network off. A seen-mark fails all three **on purpose**,
and failing all three is the proof that a fact is not the canvas's record.

So it is desk state — the home's private ledger, beside grants, passes and
spaces — written through the daemon API, which is the path the journey's rule 5
already names for exactly this class of thing. The isomorphism is untouched
because both surfaces call one route and merge with one function in
`core/seen.ts`: `GET /api/seen`, `PUT /api/seen/:canvasId`, `advanceSeen`. It is
the sixth ledger on the desk and the first that is private *for a person*
rather than to the innkeeper.

**That also answered roles.** A canvas-scoped op meets the capability check in
`POST /api/ops`, and a `read`-rung viewer is turned away by it. A viewer must
never be refused their own marks — so the seen routes ask no capability and meet
no door, because a private note about your own attention is not an edit to
anybody's canvas. The server test proves both halves at once: the same badge is
refused an `item.move` and gets its mark.

**Monotonic by construction.** `advanceSeen` is a join on each component
independently — the larger seq, the later instant — so a stale client cannot pull
a mark back, two machines racing converge whatever order their writes land in,
and a replayed desk-log entry changes nothing. Independent maxes rather than
"take the newer row whole", because a paired merge would drop one machine's
further-along seq because the other machine's visit was more recent.

**Multi-identity needed no migration.** Writes resolve through the join map, so a
new mark lands on the id the person answers to now; reads merge across every
actor the asking badge claims, which after an `actor.join` is both of them,
because a badge that claims both is what the fold required.

**Not a read receipt, and the guards are structural.** A durable record of what
you have read is a read receipt waiting to happen, and on a shared canvas that is
a harm rather than a feature: the people who read a design without commenting owe
nobody an account of it. There is no route, wire shape or derivation anywhere
that returns another actor's marks; the desk has no replication path at all; and
the legitimate version of the question — *is somebody here now* — is already
answered live, and written down nowhere, by the presence plane. The honest
caveat is stated rather than hidden: the home can see the ledger, as it can see
every op you have ever written there.

**On both surfaces.** `isocan seen` lists the canvases you have been on, most
recent first, with `moved since` on the ones that changed after you left;
`isocan seen --mark` is the one thing that writes, and the read is the default
because a verb that wrote just because you typed it is a verb you cannot use to
look. `isocan inbox --new` narrows the list to what arrived since, and the tally
line grows a fourth number beside the three per-reason ones — the 29 August
reading is why they stay separate, the Chat being twenty times the volume of
being named. In the app, opening a canvas writes the mark, and ⌘O leads with the
marks rather than with this browser's memory; that memory is still written on
every visit and is still the whole list when the daemon cannot answer.

The entry chunk paid 1,265 bytes for it — 1,379 gross, less 114 for putting the
"lately" merge behind the palette's existing `lazy()`. What is left is genuinely
eager, because opening a canvas *is* the act that writes the mark. The ceiling
moved 658,000 → 659,300 with that arithmetic beside it.
