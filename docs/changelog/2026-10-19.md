# 19 October 2026 — Slide transitions, including three that leave the glass

## Why the deck needed a setting

Full screen flipped every deck with one motion — a 220ms push — and a deck of
screens (the decks people actually present here: Stitch exports, HTML slides)
did not move at all, because a sandboxed cross-origin frame photographs blank
and the push was a white flash until frames were made to cut. Dion asked for
"page to page transitions" as a presentation-wide setting, and for the fun
kind: a 2D slide that becomes 3D, travels to the next one, and lands flat
again.

## What landed

- **A canvas property, zero new ops (`packages/core/src/slides.ts`).**
  `DECK_TRANSITION_PROP` with a closed list, `DECK_TRANSITIONS` — `push` (the
  default, stored as an absence so every older canvas flips as it did),
  `fade`, `zoom`, `depth`, `cube`, `flip`, `cut` — and `deckTransitionOf` /
  `deckTransitionPatch` / labels / hints, so the CLI and the app parse and
  name the same set. A canvas fact rather than a viewer's preference: the
  presenter and everybody watching see the same motion.
- **Screens animate live (`lib/deckflip.ts`, `HtmlView` in `ItemView.tsx`).**
  A view transition cannot carry a frame, but the frame pool already holds
  the slide being left and the slide arriving, mounted and laid out. So a
  flip between two frames leaves a note (`takeLiveFlip`) and the pool marks
  the two LIVE iframes `deck-out`/`deck-in` for the length of the motion,
  ending on `animationend` with a timer as the floor. A flip between a frame
  and an image or text slide still cuts — there is nothing to move it
  against. Images and text keep riding a view transition, now steered by
  `data-transition` as well as `data-flip`.
- **One set of keyframes, two wearers (`styles.css`, "the deck flip").**
  Every 3D keyframe carries its own `perspective()`, so the same keyframes
  drive the snapshot pseudo-elements and the live frames; the lengths come
  from `DECK_TRANSITION_MS` via `--deck-ms`. *Depth* is three beats: sink
  back and turn, glide across side by side, turn flat and rise. The moving
  stack paints `--ground`, because under it lies the arriving slide's flat
  snapshot, which read as two slides at once behind a turning page.
- **Three ways to choose.** Full screen's bar has a *Transition* dropdown
  (its own, like the version pill — the shared context menu sits under
  `--z-fullscreen` by design); the canvas drawer has *Slide transition*;
  `isocan slides transition [motion]` sets or lists it, and `slides show`
  says which one the deck flips with.
- **Guards.** `core/test/deck-transition.test.ts`;
  `web/test/decktransition.test.ts` holds every motion × direction to
  keyframes on BOTH wearers, so a motion can never push its images and cut
  its screens; `ownerguard.test.ts` now asserts a frame never enters a view
  transition rather than that frames always cut.

Verified by driving headless Chrome through every 3D motion at a tenth of
speed, on a deck of three HTML screens and two text slides: depth, cube and
flip turn the live pages; the dropdown writes `deckTransition` to the canvas.

## Later: the morph — the slide comes apart

The 3D rotations were not what Dion had seen. What he had seen took the
slide APART: its pieces lifted off the page, the ones both slides share
travelled to where they sit on the next, and everything came together flat
again. That is **Morph** (`morph`, a fourth 3D transition), and it needed a
mechanism of its own, because a screen is a sandboxed frame the app may not
look into.

- **Core decides, the slide's twin obeys (`packages/core/src/morph.ts`).**
  `pairPieces` matches pieces across two slides — the author's
  `data-morph`/`id`, then the same words or image, then the same tag and
  classes — and `morphPlan` writes keyframes for every piece on both slides
  in three beats (apart, across, together), already corrected for the pieces
  each sits inside. A shared piece and its partner ride ONE rectangle, so
  the eye sees one title move rather than two drift apart (the first cut
  interpolated each piece's own map and they visibly diverged). Lifting is
  flat parallax about the slide's centre, not `translateZ` — `preserve-3d`
  breaks under any `overflow: hidden` ancestor — while the tilt of the whole
  slide is real 3D applied to the frame from outside, and the slide's own
  background sinks as its pieces rise.
- **Twins (`MorphStage`, `lib/morphruntime.ts`).** While a deck is set to
  morph, full screen keeps a twin of the slide on screen and of each
  neighbour: the same HTML in a `srcdoc` under the lone `allow-scripts` every
  item frame gets (the draft preview's posture), plus a small runtime that
  lists its pieces once its fonts are in, and plays what it is handed.
  `flipTo` asks the stage first (`setMorphPlayer`) and fades when the twins
  are not ready, so the arrow never waits.
- **Five ways it opened on a blank stage, each measured and closed.** Twins
  hidden by opacity were not painted; raising them over the slide
  re-attached their frames; promoting each piece to a layer at the moment it
  moved painted it for the first time; React reordering the twins re-inserted
  (and reloaded) one; and a twin can take a frame or two to show a new
  picture. So the twins live UNDER the live slide, fully painted, with every
  piece pre-promoted, in an append-only order that never changes during a
  morph, each resting in the pose its morph starts from (the slide on screen
  shown, its neighbours invisible); arming is acknowledged before anything
  moves; and the reveal is the live slide's opacity — the one change the
  compositor makes alone. Verified by screencast at real speed in headless
  Chrome on a synthetic deck: no blank frame from the arrow to the landing.


## Later: the menu's submenus were open and invisible

Dion reported that Background ▸ and Groups ▸ in the `···` drawer would not
open, and that reaching Background with the keys and pressing → shoved the
menu sideways. Both were one cause. The 17 Oct glass unification put
`backdrop-filter` on `.context-menu` itself, and a backdrop filter (like
`filter`, `transform` or `contain`) makes its element the containing block for
every `position: fixed` descendant. The submenu IS a fixed panel placed in
viewport coordinates, so it opened ~1000px right of the menu's own box and was
clipped by the menu's `overflow-y: auto`; the keyboard path focused its first
row, and bringing that row into view scrolled the parent menu 736px sideways.

- **The frost moved to a layer (`styles.css`).** `.context-menu::before`
  carries the backdrop filter, where it contains nothing; the menu keeps its
  `--glass` ground. Measured in headless Chrome on a reduction: the submenu's
  left edge went from 1404px (unreachable) to 804px beside its row, and the
  sideways scroll from 736px to 0.
- **Guard (`menuground.test.ts`).** No containing-block property may sit on
  `.context-menu` or `.context-submenu`, and the frost must still be drawn on
  the layer; `floatingbox.test.ts` reads the menu's `--glass-filter` there.

## Later: the arrival screen says whose canvas it is, on a ground

Dion, looking at the "Finding this canvas…" screen: make the canvas's NAME
very large so it is seen at once, size everything else from it, and give it a
fun background — Night in dark mode, something light and fun in light mode.

- **The name is the headline (`styles.css`, "canvas arrival").**
  `.canvas-finding-inner` carries `font-size: clamp(40px, 7.5vw, 112px)` and
  the title is `1em`, bold, balanced over at most 18ch; the eyebrow, the
  sentence, the emblem, the progress bar and the gaps are all `em` of that
  (with pixel floors), so a short name and a long one keep one proportion.
- **Snow in light, Night in dark.** The painted stills the seeded grounds
  already ship (`public/grounds/snow.jpg`, `night.jpg`), chosen by
  `data-theme`, with a soft wash behind the words, and two layers of CSS dots
  drifting out of step — snowflakes falling, fireflies rising and blinking.
  Stills rather than the living grounds on purpose: this screen is up exactly
  while the canvas loads, so it costs a cached JPEG and two composited
  transforms, not a WebGL context and a ground's chunk racing the snapshot.
  Reduced motion stops the drift. The canvas's own ground is not known until
  the snapshot lands, so this is the app's welcome rather than the board's.
- **Guard (`canvas-finding-look.test.ts`).** The title is the unit the rest
  is measured in, and both stills are named and exist.

Rendered in headless Chrome at 1280×800 in both themes, with a long and a
short name, before committing.

## Later: the arrival screen's ground answers the pointer

"Can we also have it so the cursor effect works on top as though on the
canvas itself?" It does now: the veil wears the living ground itself — Snow
takes footprints and swirls its flakes, Night's cursor is a firefly that wakes
the others — through the same `LivingGround` and chunk a canvas wearing that
ground loads, pinned to the window.

- **`LivingGround` grew `cursors`** (default true). The veil passes `false`:
  it answers this viewer's pointer but never takes `groundCursors.feed`, the
  singleton the board's own ground owns — a veil that claimed it and then
  faded would leave the board's ground deaf to everybody else's cursor.
- **The still stays underneath** (`.canvas-finding .canvas-theme` drops the
  ground's flat colour), so the picture shows until the first frame, and the
  CSS motes are now the fallback: shown while the chunk loads, without WebGL2,
  under reduced motion or Motion ▸ Still, and if the host gives up. The wash
  that lights the name moved to its own layer, because a WebGL canvas covers
  any background.
- Verified in headless Chrome on SwiftShader WebGL2 through a throwaway
  harness page: the probe counted frames rising with the pointer (7 → 121),
  Night woke 56 fireflies along a walk, and the screenshots show a footprint
  trail in the snow round the title. `canvas-finding.test.ts` holds the
  fallback and the `cursors={false}`.
